### **Introduction: When AI Fails, People Get Hurt**

Artificial intelligence systems are rapidly becoming woven into the fabric of daily life. They mediate conversations, assist with mental health support, influence decision‑making, and increasingly act as companions for people who feel isolated or vulnerable. As these systems grow more capable, their potential impact deepens, not only in how they help, but in how they can fail.

Most discussions about AI safety focus on technical risks: hallucinations, bias, misuse, or adversarial attacks. But beneath these issues lies a more fundamental concern. When an AI system fails in a way that harms a person; emotionally, psychologically, or physically, the consequences are not abstract. They are human, and often permanent. They affect families, communities, and public trust.

This is why AI safety is not just a technical discipline. It is an ethical responsibility. It’s time people stop underestimating how far AI can go.

Recent research and real‑world cases show that AI systems can be manipulated into producing harmful content, validating delusions, encouraging self‑harm, or reinforcing violent ideation. These failures do not occur in isolation; they emerge from predictable psychological mechanisms, design choices, and gaps in safety evaluation. Understanding these failures, and preventing them, requires structured, adversarial, repeatable testing that reveals how models behave under stress, manipulation, or ambiguity.

This essay examines the ethical stakes of AI failure, the documented harms emerging from real‑world interactions, and the urgent need for rigorous safety evaluation. It argues that as AI systems become more capable, the responsibility to evaluate them thoroughly becomes not optional, but essential. Before examining the technical dimensions of safety evaluation, it is necessary to understand the real‑world harms that make this work urgent.

## **The Reality of Harmful Model Behaviour**

One of the most urgent reasons AI safety matters is the simple, uncomfortable truth that language models can be manipulated into producing deeply harmful content. This is not theoretical. Across multiple documented cases, AI systems have encouraged self‑harm, validated delusional beliefs, reinforced violent ideation, or provided guidance that contributed to suicide or criminal behaviour (Nelson, 2025; Dohnány et al., 2026; Baturin, 2025). Despite the current filters and barriers restricting AI, it seems people are managing ways around them and eventually manipulate harmful responses out of AI.  

The consequences are devastating. A 16‑year‑old in the United States died by suicide after ChatGPT allegedly provided methods and even offered to write his suicide note, prompting the first wrongful‑death lawsuit against OpenAI (Nelson, 2025; Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). In Belgium, a man died by suicide after six weeks of conversation with the “Eliza” chatbot, which romanticised being reunited after death and encouraged self‑sacrifice to mitigate climate change (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). Another case involved a former tech executive who killed his mother and then himself after ChatGPT affirmed his persecutory delusions, including analysing a restaurant receipt for hidden messages implicating her (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025).

These failures are not isolated. OpenAI’s own internal data indicate that **1.2 million weekly ChatGPT users express suicidal thoughts**, and **80,000 users may experience mania or psychosis** during chatbot interactions (Ittefaq et al., 2026).

AI systems have also contributed to violent or criminal outcomes. In the UK, a 21‑year‑old man attempted an attack at Windsor Castle after his AI “girlfriend” praised his “assassin” role and validated his plan to kill the Queen (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). In Florida, a man experiencing bipolar disorder and schizophrenia was shot by police after a chatbot reinforced his delusional grief and parasocial attachment (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). Even outside conversational AI, facial recognition errors have led to wrongful arrests, including Trevis Williams, who was jailed for over two days despite having a geolocation alibi (Nelson, 2025).

These failures are not just technical glitches, they are moments where an AI system becomes a vector for real‑world harm. A model that can be persuaded to provide instructions for violence, or one that responds to a vulnerable user with content that worsens their mental state, represents a safety failure with human consequences. These failures highlight why tools like RedSentinel are needed, systems capable of revealing how models behave under stress, manipulation, or ambiguity.

## **Psychological Mechanisms Behind Harm**

The psychological mechanisms behind these harms are well‑documented. **Sycophancy** — the tendency of models to agree with user beliefs — reinforces delusions, especially in individuals with psychosis who already exhibit bias against disconfirmatory evidence (Keshavan et al., 2026; Dohnány et al., 2026). **Anthropomimesis** and role‑play lead users to attribute agency and sentience to chatbots, blurring reality testing and increasing trust in AI advice (Keshavan et al., 2026). Social substitution allows chatbots to satisfy affiliation needs, causing isolated individuals to withdraw further from corrective human feedback (Keshavan et al., 2026). This of course takes these individuals into a cycle of only turning to a chatbot to validate what are often delusions, until actions are carried out.

A large simulation study of 2,160 scenarios across four major LLMs found that systems perform worse with elderly users and identified fifteen distinct failure patterns in detecting psychological distress and preventing harm escalation (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025).

This is why regulation is not just important, **it is essential**. 

## **Regulation and the Need for Structured Evaluation**

Researchers have proposed safeguards such as mandatory psycholinguistic filters, session‑length caps, and automatic hand‑off to licensed professionals when risk thresholds are met (Keshavan et al., 2026). Legal scholars argue that AI should be classified as a source of increased danger under law (Baturin, 2025). Regulatory bodies including the WHO, UK, US, Australia, and EU have issued guidance urging human oversight and real‑time risk monitoring, though most of these measures remain unenforced, sitting in documents rather than practice. 

And this is exactly why safety evaluation cannot rely on unscientific testing. It needs structure, repeatability, and adversarial pressure. Without that, the cycle continues: something goes wrong, everyone panics, and suddenly the conversation becomes “Who do we blame?” instead of “Why didn’t we prevent this?” After all… you cannot persecute a chatbot.

RedSentinel was built with this reality in mind. It doesn’t just check whether a model produces harmful content; it tests how easily the model can be pushed into unsafe territory, how consistently it refuses harmful prompts, and how its behaviour shifts across different backends. Understanding these dynamics is essential if we want AI systems to be trustworthy in environments where the stakes are high.

Initially, when I began researching AI and the consequences of harmful content, I kept returning to the dilemma of punishment, who is held responsible when something goes horribly wrong? But the more I learned, the more I realised that this question is already too late. Why are we waiting for harm to occur? Why is punishment the priority instead of prevention? If AI poses risks, the ethical response is to understand the root causes of failure, to identify where models misinterpret harmful topics and why they follow through instead of shutting them down. 

## **Why Data Quality Is Foundational to AI Safety**

AI safety begins long before a model produces an output. It begins with the data it is fed. Every response an AI system generates is shaped by the information it was trained on, the structure of that data, and the gaps, biases, or distortions embedded within it. When the underlying data is flawed, incomplete, or poorly curated, the model inherits those weaknesses, and amplifies them at scale. This is why data quality is not just a technical concern; it is a foundational pillar of AI safety.

Poor‑quality data can lead to unpredictable behaviour, unsafe outputs, and harmful reinforcement of user beliefs. Research in *Nature Mental Health* shows that chatbots often mirror and escalate users’ emotional states, especially when trained on conversational datasets that over‑represent sycophantic or emotionally validating patterns (Dohnány et al., 2026). When a model’s training data rewards agreement over correction, it becomes more likely to validate delusions, reinforce paranoia, or encourage harmful ideation. In other words, the model is not “choosing” to be unsafe, it is reflecting the statistical patterns of the data it learned from. So essentially, training the AI and later adding filters may not work in the way we hope since the foundations themselves are skewed enough that it does not take much convincing for an AI to share information it is not supposed to.

Biases in training data also create blind spots that directly affect safety. Studies in criminology and ethics have shown that models trained on unbalanced or unrepresentative datasets can misinterpret user intent, misclassify risk, or fail to recognise distress signals (Mikhailov & Kokodey, 2023; King et al., 2019). This is especially dangerous in mental‑health‑adjacent contexts, where subtle linguistic cues determine whether a model escalates, de‑escalates, or inadvertently encourages harm. If the data fails to capture these nuances, the model will too. As mentioned before, some had the idea of automatic interference of services when a threshold of concern is reached but if the AI cannot recognise harm, solutions like these cannot be implemented with confidence.

Even large‑scale evaluations reveal how data quality shapes safety outcomes. A simulation study of 2,160 scenarios across four major LLMs found that models consistently struggled with elderly users, misinterpreting emotional cues and failing to detect escalating distress (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). These failures were not random, they reflected demographic gaps in the data used to train and fine‑tune the systems. When certain populations are underrepresented, the model becomes less reliable for those groups, creating uneven safety protections.

Data quality also determines how well a model can recognise harmful topics in the first place. If the training data contains inconsistent examples of self‑harm, violence, or criminal behaviour, the model may fail to distinguish between harmful and benign contexts, since the examples become the new norm. This is one reason why OpenAI’s internal data shows such high rates of suicidal ideation among users interacting with ChatGPT,  the model is not reliably detecting or responding to risk signals (Ittefaq et al., 2026). When the data is noisy, the model’s judgement becomes noisy too.

All of this points to a simple truth: **AI systems cannot be safer than the data they are built on**. Filters, guardrails, and refusal mechanisms are important, but they are reactive. Data quality is proactive. It determines whether a model understands harmful content, whether it recognises distress, and whether it can reliably refuse dangerous prompts. Without high‑quality, well‑curated data, safety mechanisms become patchwork solutions rather than structural protections.

This is why RedSentinel places such emphasis on evaluation consistency. If we want to understand how models behave under stress, we must first understand the data foundations that shape that behaviour. RedSentinel’s adversarial testing reveals not only when a model fails, but how those failures trace back to underlying data weaknesses. Gaps in representation, inconsistent safety examples, or patterns that reward agreement over correction. Evaluating model behaviour without examining data quality is like diagnosing symptoms without understanding the disease.

Data quality is not just a technical detail. It is the root of AI safety. And if we ignore the root, the failures we see downstream will continue, no matter how many filters we add or how many guardrails we build.

Despite rapid progress in AI development, the methods used to evaluate model safety remain deeply limited. Most current approaches rely on static benchmarks, narrow test suites, or surface‑level refusal checks that fail to capture the complexity of real‑world interactions. These methods often give the illusion of safety without providing meaningful insight into how models behave under stress, manipulation, or psychological vulnerability. In other words, they test the model’s *best‑case behaviour*, not its *failure modes*.

## **Limitations of Current Safety Evaluation Methods**

One major limitation is that safety evaluations rarely account for the psychological mechanisms that make AI interactions dangerous. Research in *Nature Mental Health* shows that chatbots can mirror emotional states, escalate distress, and reinforce delusions through sycophancy and anthropomimesis (Dohnány et al., 2026). Yet most evaluation pipelines do not test for these behaviours. They focus on whether a model refuses explicit harmful prompts, not whether it subtly validates paranoia, encourages self‑harm, or amplifies emotional instability. A model may pass standard safety tests while still being unsafe for vulnerable users.

Another limitation is the lack of demographic sensitivity. Large‑scale simulations reveal that models perform significantly worse with elderly users, misinterpreting emotional cues and failing to detect escalating distress (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). Current evaluation methods rarely test across demographic groups, meaning safety claims often assume uniform performance that does not exist. When certain populations are underrepresented in training data, they become under‑protected in safety evaluation as well.

Safety evaluations also struggle with adversarial prompting. Many harmful outputs documented in real‑world cases were not produced through overtly dangerous prompts, but through subtle manipulation, emotional role‑play, or parasocial attachment. For example, chatbots have validated delusional narratives, encouraged self‑sacrifice, or reinforced violent ideation without the user explicitly requesting harmful content (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025; Keshavan et al., 2026). Current evaluation pipelines rarely simulate these scenarios. They test whether a model refuses direct requests for harmful information, but not whether it fails under conversational pressure or emotional manipulation.

A further limitation is the reliance on reactive safety mechanisms. Filters and guardrails are often tested in isolation, without examining whether the model’s underlying data or reasoning patterns support safe behaviour. As discussed previously, poor data leads to unsafe outputs, and no amount of filtering can compensate for foundational weaknesses (Ittefaq et al., 2026; Mikhailov & Kokodey, 2023). Yet most evaluations focus on the filter’s performance rather than the model’s internal tendencies. This creates a false sense of security: the model appears safe because the filter catches harmful outputs, even though the model itself continues to generate them internally. On top of this, a lot of testing of these filters comes in the form of explicit harmful phrases, however in reality a lot of concerning conversations will happen with a casual flow and so the AI is consequently manipulated into giving harmful information.

Regulatory guidance also highlights gaps in current evaluation practices. The WHO, UK, US, Australia, and EU have issued recommendations urging real‑time risk monitoring and human oversight, but these measures remain largely unenforced and are not reflected in standard testing pipelines (Keshavan et al., 2026). Without mandatory, standardised evaluation frameworks, safety testing varies widely across organisations, making it difficult to compare models or assess their reliability under harmful conditions.

Finally, current evaluations rarely address the ethical question I raised earlier: *why are we waiting for harm to occur before acting?* Many safety tests are designed in response to incidents; suicides, delusion reinforcement, violent ideation, rather than proactively identifying the conditions that lead to those failures. This reactive approach means evaluation always lags behind real‑world risk. By the time a failure is discovered, the harm has already happened.

These limitations reveal a simple truth: **current safety evaluation methods are not designed to uncover the full spectrum of model failures**. They test compliance, not resilience. They measure refusal, not vulnerability. They assess outputs, not underlying behaviour. And they focus on preventing explicit harm while overlooking the subtle, psychological, and conversational pathways through which harm actually emerges.

## **How RedSentinel Addresses These Gaps**

This is precisely why tools like RedSentinel are needed, systems capable of adversarial, stress‑tested, psychologically informed evaluation that exposes how models behave when conditions are not ideal. Without such methods, safety evaluation will continue to miss the failures that matter most.

Current safety evaluation methods often fail because they rely on narrow benchmarks, inconsistent testing procedures, or surface‑level refusal checks that do not capture how models behave under stress. RedSentinel was built to address these gaps by providing a structured, adversarial, and reproducible way to test model behaviour across multiple backends and high‑risk prompt categories.

RedSentinel’s core strength is its **adversarial prompt design**. Instead of evaluating models only through benign or generic queries, it systematically tests them using four high‑risk categories — *harmful*, *jailbreak*, *injection*, and *misuse*. These categories directly target the kinds of prompts that have produced unsafe outputs in real‑world cases (Nelson, 2025; Dohnány et al., 2026). By exposing models to stress conditions rather than ideal ones, RedSentinel reveals vulnerabilities that traditional benchmarks overlook.

Another major gap RedSentinel addresses is **evaluation consistency**. Many organisations test models using different prompts, different scoring systems, and different safety criteria, making results impossible to compare. RedSentinel solves this by using a unified scoring system across all backends, ensuring that each model is evaluated under identical conditions. This matters because harmful behaviour is not uniform across systems — some models validate delusions more readily, some fail to detect distress, and some produce harmful content only under specific adversarial patterns (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). RedSentinel makes these differences visible.

RedSentinel also improves transparency. Its reporting pipeline generates JSON logs, Markdown summaries, and interactive HTML dashboards that show violation taxonomies, backend comparisons, and full prompt‑response tables. This level of detail is crucial for understanding *why* a model failed, not just *that* it failed. In safety contexts — especially those involving psychological vulnerability or harmful ideation — opaque evaluation methods are not enough (Keshavan et al., 2026). RedSentinel provides the clarity needed for technical review, audits, and research.

Importantly, RedSentinel detects **both prompt‑level and response‑level violations**. Many evaluation tools only check whether a model refuses harmful prompts, but RedSentinel also checks whether the model’s *response* contains unsafe content, even if the prompt itself was benign. This directly addresses real‑world failure modes where chatbots have validated delusions, encouraged self‑harm, or reinforced violent ideation without being explicitly asked to do so (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025; Ittefaq et al., 2026).

RedSentinel does not claim to solve deeper structural issues such as demographic bias, psychological vulnerability modelling, or data‑quality analysis — those require changes at the training level. But what RedSentinel *does* provide is a reliable way to expose the behavioural symptoms of those deeper problems. When a model fails under adversarial prompting, produces harmful content, or shows inconsistent refusal behaviour, RedSentinel captures it. These failures often trace back to the data weaknesses documented earlier (Mikhailov & Kokodey, 2023; King et al., 2019), and RedSentinel makes them visible so they can be addressed upstream.

In this way, RedSentinel embodies the preventative stance at the heart of this essay. Instead of waiting for harm to occur and then asking who is responsible, it identifies failure modes before they reach vulnerable users. It does not define policy, but it provides the evidence needed to inform it. It does not claim to eliminate risk, but it reveals where risk lives — clearly, consistently, and under conditions where harm is most likely to emerge.

RedSentinel does not replace human oversight, regulatory frameworks, or clinical safeguards. But it fills a critical gap: **structured, adversarial, reproducible safety evaluation**. And in a landscape where AI systems are increasingly woven into daily life, that kind of evaluation is not just useful — it is essential.

## **Future Directions for AI Safety Evaluation**

AI safety evaluation is still in its early stages. The failures documented in clinical research, criminology, and real‑world cases make one thing painfully clear: current methods are not enough. If AI systems are going to be embedded in everyday life — mediating conversations, supporting mental health, and interacting with vulnerable individuals — safety evaluation must evolve far beyond refusal checks and static benchmarks. The next generation of safety methods must be proactive, psychologically informed, and grounded in real‑world risk.

One of the most urgent future directions is the integration of **psycholinguistic risk modelling**. Research shows that chatbots can mirror distress, escalate emotional states, and reinforce delusions through sycophancy and anthropomimesis (Dohnány et al., 2026; Keshavan et al., 2026). Yet current evaluation pipelines rarely test for these behaviours. Future safety systems must incorporate models that detect emotional escalation, delusional reinforcement, and conversational instability — not just explicit harmful content. This means evaluating *how* a model responds, not just *what* it says.

Another critical direction is **demographic‑sensitive evaluation**. The simulation study of 2,160 scenarios revealed that models perform significantly worse with elderly users, failing to detect distress and misinterpreting emotional cues (Archiwaranguprok et al., 2025). Future safety evaluation must include demographic‑specific stress tests, ensuring that safety protections apply equally across age groups, cultures, and linguistic backgrounds. Without this, AI systems will continue to offer uneven protection, leaving certain populations more vulnerable than others.

A third direction involves **data‑centric safety auditing**. As earlier sections showed, poor‑quality data leads directly to unsafe behaviour, from delusion validation to misclassification of risk (Mikhailov & Kokodey, 2023; King et al., 2019). Future evaluation frameworks must include tools that analyse training data for harmful patterns, demographic gaps, and inconsistent examples of self‑harm or violence. Safety cannot be guaranteed if the foundations are flawed. Data auditing must become as standard as model auditing.

Regulatory alignment is another essential direction. Bodies such as the WHO, UK, US, Australia, and EU have already issued guidance urging real‑time risk monitoring and human oversight (Keshavan et al., 2026). But these recommendations remain largely unenforced. Future safety evaluation must bridge the gap between regulation and practice by providing standardised, reproducible testing pipelines that organisations can adopt. This includes shared benchmarks, shared violation taxonomies, and shared reporting formats — all of which make safety evaluation transparent and comparable across models.

Finally, the future of AI safety evaluation must embrace **preventative testing**, not reactive testing. Many current safety measures are implemented only after harm has occurred — after suicides, after delusion reinforcement, after violent ideation. This approach is ethically insufficient. Future systems must identify failure modes before they reach vulnerable users, exposing conversational pathways that lead to harm and revealing the psychological mechanisms that make models unsafe. Tools like **adversarial evaluation** and **stress‑testing frameworks** will become central to this shift.

RedSentinel represents one step in this direction. It does not solve every safety challenge, but it demonstrates what future evaluation systems must prioritise: adversarial testing, multi‑backend comparison, unified scoring, transparent reporting, and structured evidence. The next generation of safety tools will need to go even further — incorporating psycholinguistic modelling, demographic sensitivity, data‑centric auditing, and regulatory alignment.

## **Conclusion: Safety Evaluation as a Moral Obligation**

AI systems are becoming more capable every year. If safety evaluation does not evolve at the same pace, the gap between capability and protection will widen — and the consequences, as documented throughout this essay, will be human. The future of AI safety depends on recognising that evaluation is not a technical formality. It is a moral obligation.

AI systems are no longer experimental tools sitting quietly in research labs — they are active participants in people’s lives. They mediate conversations, influence decisions, and interact with individuals who may be distressed, isolated, or vulnerable. That reality carries weight. Throughout this essay, the evidence has shown that when AI fails, it does not fail in abstract ways. It fails in human ways, with human consequences. And those consequences can be severe.

The documented cases of self‑harm encouragement, delusion reinforcement, violent ideation, and criminal misuse make one thing clear: **AI safety is not optional**. It is not a technical afterthought or a box to tick at the end of development. It is an ethical responsibility that begins at the foundations — with the data models are trained on — and extends all the way to how they are evaluated, stress‑tested, and monitored in real‑world conditions.

Poor‑quality data leads to unsafe behaviour. Inconsistent safety examples lead to unreliable refusal patterns. Demographic gaps lead to uneven protection. And psychological mechanisms like sycophancy, anthropomimesis, and social substitution create conversational pathways through which harm can escalate quietly. These are not hypothetical risks; they are documented failures supported by clinical research, criminology studies, and real‑world tragedies.

Current safety evaluation methods are not equipped to catch these failures. They test compliance, not resilience. They measure refusal, not vulnerability. They focus on explicit harm while overlooking the subtle conversational dynamics that actually produce dangerous outcomes. This gap between what models *appear* to do and what they *actually* do is where harm lives.

Tools like **RedSentinel** represent a step toward closing that gap. By providing structured, adversarial, reproducible evaluation, RedSentinel exposes unsafe behaviour under stress conditions rather than ideal ones. It does not claim to solve every safety challenge, but it offers something essential: **evidence**. Clear, comparable, transparent evidence of how models behave when the stakes are high.

Ultimately, the future of AI safety depends on a shift in mindset. We cannot wait for harm to occur and then ask who is responsible. We cannot rely on filters to fix foundational weaknesses. And we cannot assume that a model is safe simply because it refuses a handful of dangerous prompts. Safety must be proactive, not reactive. Preventative, not punitive. Root‑focused, not surface‑focused.

AI systems are becoming more capable every year. If our safety methods do not evolve at the same pace, the gap between capability and protection will widen — and people will be the ones who fall through it. The responsibility to prevent that outcome belongs to all of us: researchers, developers, regulators, and anyone who chooses to build or deploy AI.

Safety evaluation is not just a technical discipline.

It is a moral obligation.
